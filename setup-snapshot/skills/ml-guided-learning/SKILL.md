# Guided ML learning

Choose the smallest concept that unlocks the current research task. Recover the learner's demonstrated level and own questions. Do not turn the full skill catalog into prerequisite homework.

1. Explain the mechanism in familiar terms, then connect it to the actual code, tensor, equation, or experimental outcome. Define new notation and units before using them.
2. Invite one concrete prediction or small modification through the supported user-input channel. Leave the answer open; do not write a prediction on the learner's behalf. Continue independent engineering that does not spoil the exercise.
3. Implement or inspect a minimal example with roles clear: assistant-written, learner-written, or jointly modified. Relate lines to the mechanism rather than narrating syntax alone.
4. Measure the output, compare with the actual prediction if provided, and investigate disagreement. Distinguish a teaching example from research evidence.
5. Ask the learner to explain a changed case or the reason for the outcome. Record demonstrated participation accurately; silence or reading an explanation does not demonstrate mastery.

Suitable bridges include Python functions → behavior checks, vectors/matrices → batch shapes, derivatives → gradients, probability → cross-entropy, sampling → uncertainty, and retrieval context → information boundaries. Introduce calculus or architecture detail only when it helps the current step.

Preserve the user's journal verbatim. Assistant explanations, exercise prompts, and pending checkpoints belong in designated private teaching/session notes. Never fill in predictions, reflections, completed exercises, or understanding for the user.

Explain and review before a phase transition under the project's rules. Repair a missing prerequisite while independent work continues; do not either block all research on coursework or pretend the learner has caught up. Keep public participation claims tied to observable contributions.
